## [01:10] Kinetic Theory and Reaction Rates

The rate constant in a chemical reaction is fundamentally dependent on temperature, as described by the Arrhenius equation. The core relationship states that for every increase of ten degrees (whether in Celsius or Kelvin), the reaction rate typically doubles.

This principle applies universally because adding $10^\circ C$ and increasing the absolute temperature ($K$) by the same amount produces an identical effect on molecular energy distribution, thereby doubling the fraction of molecules exceeding the activation threshold. If this exponential growth continues—where a ten-degree rise from 25°C doubles the rate to reach 4k at 35°C, then again for 8k at 45°C—the underlying mathematical model becomes increasingly significant.

The Arrhenius equation connects these variables as:
$k = A \cdot e^{-(E_a / RT)}$
In a linearized form used to plot reaction rates, the gradient represents: $\ln(k) = -E_a/(2.303 R T)$
The constant 2.303 arises from converting between natural logarithms and base-10 logarithms in practical calculations.
